He Took on Red Bull Over Its "Gives You Wings" Slogan. Here's What Really Happened.

Imagine buying an energy drink because you believe the advertising.

You crack open a can, take a sip, and wait for that extra boost of energy.

But nothing crazy happens.

You don't suddenly become a superhero. You don't grow wings. And you definitely don't start flying around your neighborhood.

Now, imagine taking one of the world's biggest energy drink companies to court over the claims behind its advertising.

Sounds ridiculous, right?

Well, something surprisingly close to that actually happened.

In 2014, Red Bull agreed to a settlement worth up to $13 million to resolve a class-action lawsuit over its advertising.

And no, this wasn't a case of one guy walking into court demanding a pair of wings.

The real story involved claims about energy, performance, consumer protection, and a legal fight that attracted attention across the country.

But here's the part most people get wrong.

One man did not personally walk away with $13 million.

So what actually happened? Why did Red Bull agree to the settlement? And how much money could ordinary customers really receive?

To understand the story, we need to go back to the rise of one of the most recognizable brands in the world.

PART ONE: HOW RED BULL TOOK OVER THE WORLD

Today, Red Bull is much more than an energy drink.

It's associated with Formula One racing, extreme sports, professional athletes, and some of the most recognizable advertising campaigns on the planet.

But the company didn't become a global powerhouse overnight.

Red Bull launched in Austria in 1987 after entrepreneur Dietrich Mateschitz became interested in energy drinks that were already popular in parts of Asia.

He saw an opportunity to bring a similar product to Western consumers.

But selling another drink wasn't enough.

Red Bull needed an identity.

The company built its brand around energy, excitement, performance, and pushing beyond your limits.

Then came the slogan that millions of people would eventually recognize:

"Red Bull gives you wings."

The advertising was memorable, humorous, and easy to understand.

Nobody seriously expected a pair of wings to sprout from their back after drinking a can.

That wasn't the point.

The slogan was a metaphor for feeling energized and ready to take on more.

And it worked.

Red Bull became a major player in the energy drink market, connecting its product to everything from studying late at night to professional sports.

But as the brand grew, so did the importance of its advertising claims.

And eventually, some consumers began questioning whether the company was promising more than the evidence supported.

That question would become the center of a legal dispute.

PART TWO: THE LAWSUIT THAT CHALLENGED RED BULL

In January 2013, a man named Benjamin Careathers filed a lawsuit against Red Bull North America.

The lawsuit challenged aspects of the company's advertising and marketing.

And this is where the story gets interesting.

Despite the jokes that spread online, the case wasn't about someone literally expecting to grow wings.

The central issue was whether Red Bull's advertising made misleading claims about the benefits of its product.

The allegations focused on representations about energy, concentration, and performance, including claims suggesting benefits beyond what consumers might get from ordinary sources of caffeine.

Careathers argued that the company's marketing created an impression that wasn't adequately supported.

In other words, the dispute wasn't really about wings.

It was about what the company was telling customers they could expect from the drink.

And that distinction matters.

In the United States, companies are allowed to use humor, exaggeration, and creative language in advertising.

But advertising can cross a legal line if it makes misleading claims about a product's actual benefits.

The challenge is figuring out where that line is.

A slogan like "Red Bull gives you wings" sounds like obvious exaggeration.

But claims about concentration, energy, and physical performance can raise more specific questions.

What exactly is being promised?

What evidence supports those claims?

And would an ordinary consumer reasonably interpret the advertising as suggesting a measurable benefit?

Those were the kinds of issues behind the dispute.

And Careathers wasn't the only person involved in legal action over Red Bull's advertising.

PART THREE: THIS WAS BIGGER THAN ONE PERSON

The case became part of a broader legal dispute involving multiple consumers.

Another lawsuit, brought by David Wolfe and other plaintiffs, was transferred to federal court in New York in October 2013 to be consolidated with related litigation.

That detail is important because it changes the way we should understand the story.

Online, the case is often described as one determined guy taking on a giant corporation and winning $13 million.

But that's not what happened.

This was a class-action lawsuit.

A class action allows one or more people to represent a larger group of consumers who have similar legal claims.

Instead of every customer filing a separate lawsuit, the claims can be handled together under certain legal requirements.

In this case, the dispute involved consumers who had purchased Red Bull products in the United States.

Think about the scale.

One customer might buy a single can at a gas station.

Another might drink Red Bull every week.

Someone else might buy it before work, before a workout, or during a late-night study session.

Most customers probably weren't thinking about the legal meaning of the company's advertising.

They simply wanted an energy drink.

But when a lawsuit challenges claims made to millions of potential customers, the consequences can extend far beyond the person who originally filed it.

That's why the case attracted so much attention.

Still, filing a lawsuit doesn't automatically prove that the allegations are true.

And Red Bull had its own side of the story.

PART FOUR: RED BULL'S RESPONSE

Red Bull denied the allegations of false advertising.

The company maintained that its marketing and labeling were truthful and accurate and disputed claims that it had violated the law.

That's an important distinction.

A settlement doesn't automatically mean a company admits wrongdoing.

And in this case, Red Bull did not admit liability as part of the agreement.

But legal disputes can be expensive, complicated, and unpredictable.

They can require years of work from lawyers, extensive document reviews, and ongoing court proceedings.

There can also be reputational consequences.

Imagine the headlines.

A company famous for telling people that its drink gives them wings is now fighting a lawsuit over its advertising.

Even if the slogan is intended as a joke, the story is easy for the public to understand.

And once a dispute becomes a major news story, the company has another problem to consider: how much time, money, and attention should it spend continuing the fight?

Sometimes, the parties decide that settling a case is preferable to continuing litigation.

That's what happened here.

But the settlement came with a headline-grabbing number.

Thirteen million dollars.

PART FIVE: THE $13 MILLION SETTLEMENT

In July 2014, Red Bull agreed to a settlement with a total value of up to $13 million.

The agreement was granted preliminary approval in September 2014 and received final court approval in May 2015.

And this is where the internet version of the story often goes off the rails.

The $13 million wasn't a personal payout to Benjamin Careathers.

It was the maximum overall value of a settlement designed to cover eligible consumers who fell within the agreement's terms.

Under the settlement, eligible people who had purchased qualifying Red Bull products in the United States between January 1, 2002, and October 3, 2014, could submit claims.

They could choose between two forms of compensation.

One option was a $10 cash payment.

The other was free Red Bull products with an advertised retail value of $15.

And according to the published settlement notice, consumers generally didn't have to provide ordinary proof of purchase to submit a claim.

Of course, eligibility rules and other conditions still applied.

But the basic idea was simple.

Instead of fighting over every individual purchase, the settlement created a process through which eligible consumers could request compensation.

That made the story much bigger than one man's complaint.

It also explains why the settlement total was so large.

The money wasn't meant for one person.

It was intended to resolve claims involving a much larger group of consumers.

And that leads to another question.

If millions of people could submit claims, did everyone actually receive ten dollars?

Not necessarily.

PART SIX: WHAT HAPPENED TO THE MONEY?

Here's where the story gets even more complicated.

A settlement worth $13 million doesn't mean every eligible consumer automatically receives a fixed amount.

The agreement had a financial limit, and the final distribution depended on the settlement's terms and the number of valid claims.

Millions of consumers reportedly submitted claims.

When that many people seek compensation from a limited fund, the final amount available to each person can be affected by the way the agreement is administered.

Legal discussions of the settlement reported that some cash payments ultimately came to approximately $4.23, rather than the original $10 figure.

That detail is one reason the story became such a popular example of the difference between a headline and what an individual consumer actually receives.

Think about it.

The headline says $13 million.

A person sees that number and imagines someone becoming a millionaire overnight.

But the reality is very different.

The settlement covered a large group of consumers, and the money had to be distributed under the agreement's rules.

The result wasn't one person collecting a massive check.

It was a legal settlement involving many consumers, with individual compensation that could be much smaller than the headline suggested.

That doesn't automatically mean the settlement was pointless.

It resolved the claims covered by the agreement and included commitments concerning the substantiation of certain future claims about the product's functional benefits.

But it does show why the details matter.

A $13 million settlement and a $13 million personal payout are two completely different things.

And there's one more part of the story that deserves attention.

The lawyers.

PART SEVEN: THE LAWYERS AND THE FIGHT OVER FEES

Class-action lawsuits can involve enormous amounts of legal work.

Attorneys have to investigate claims, prepare legal arguments, review evidence, negotiate terms, and represent the interests of the proposed class.

That work can take years.

And in a settlement involving millions of dollars, legal fees can become a major issue of their own.

The Red Bull case was no exception.

The agreement included provisions for legal fees and administrative costs.

The requested attorneys' fees were also reviewed during the court's consideration of the settlement.

The court ultimately reduced the requested attorneys' fees by $1.2 million.

That detail reveals something important about class-action settlements.

Even after the parties agree on a deal, the court may still have to review the agreement and determine whether its terms are fair and reasonable.

The judge isn't simply there to rubber-stamp whatever the company and lawyers decide.

The court has a role in evaluating the settlement, including how it affects the people the lawsuit is supposed to represent.

And that brings us back to the biggest misconception about the Red Bull case.

It wasn't simply a story about someone demanding money because a drink didn't give him wings.

It was a dispute over advertising claims, consumer protection, and the terms under which a large group of consumers could receive compensation.

The wings were the punchline.

The legal questions were the real story.

PART EIGHT: DID RED BULL ACTUALLY LOSE?

So, did Red Bull lose?

The most accurate answer is that the company agreed to settle the class-action litigation.

The settlement received final court approval in 2015.

But Red Bull did not admit wrongdoing as part of that agreement.

That means it's misleading to describe the case as a court definitively proving every allegation against the company.

The lawsuit raised claims about the company's advertising.

Red Bull disputed those claims.

The parties reached an agreement, and the court approved the settlement.

Those are the facts we can establish without turning the story into something it wasn't.

Still, the case illustrates a broader point about advertising.

Companies spend enormous amounts of money persuading people to buy their products.

They use memorable slogans, celebrity endorsements, emotional storytelling, and carefully crafted images.

Most consumers understand that advertising often exaggerates.

But there can be a difference between a playful slogan and a claim about what a product can actually do.

When companies make specific claims about performance or health-related benefits, those claims may face greater scrutiny.

And when a product is sold to millions of people, even a small dispute can become a major legal issue.

That's what made this case so fascinating.

A familiar energy drink became the center of a legal battle over what its advertising meant and what consumers were entitled to expect.

And the result was a settlement worth millions.

THE ENDING: SO WHO REALLY GOT THE $13 MILLION?

Let's answer the question that started this whole story.

Did one man sue Red Bull and personally walk away with $13 million?

No.

Benjamin Careathers filed a lawsuit in 2013, but the litigation became part of a broader class-action dispute.

In 2014, Red Bull agreed to a settlement with a total value of up to $13 million.

The settlement received final court approval in 2015.

Eligible consumers could submit claims for cash or free products, but individual compensation was not the same as the overall settlement amount.

And Red Bull did not admit wrongdoing as part of the agreement.

So the real story isn't that one man demanded wings and became a millionaire.

It's that a legal dispute over advertising led to a multimillion-dollar settlement involving a much larger group of consumers.

And there's a lesson here that goes beyond energy drinks.

Whenever a company makes a promise about its product, it's worth asking a simple question:

Is that promise just clever marketing, or is it a claim the company should be able to prove?

Because sometimes, the difference between the two can become a very expensive problem.
